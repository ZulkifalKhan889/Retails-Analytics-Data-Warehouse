## Measures table and core measures (DAX)

- What I did: created a `_Measures` table and six measures: Total Revenue,
  Total Orders, Total Items Sold, Avg Order Value, Unique Customers, Total Freight
- Why: a measure recalculates for whatever the dashboard is filtered to.
  If I click state SP, Total Revenue shows only SP, and Avg Order Value
  becomes SP revenue / SP orders. A number stored in a SQL view can't do that.
  I keep all measures in one table so they're easy to find.
- How: Home > Enter data > named it _Measures > Load. Then selected _Measures >
  Home > New measure for each one:
    Total Revenue = SUM(vw_sales[price])
    Total Orders = DISTINCTCOUNT(vw_sales[order_id])
    Total Items Sold = COUNTROWS(vw_sales)
    Avg Order Value = DIVIDE([Total Revenue], [Total Orders])
    Unique Customers = DISTINCTCOUNT(vw_sales[customer_unique_id])
    Total Freight = SUM(vw_sales[freight_value])
  - DISTINCTCOUNT counts an order once even if it has 3 items
  - DIVIDE returns blank instead of an error when there is nothing to divide

### Interpretation of what bar chart and key takeaways explain
- The bar chart, in plain English
- Each bar is one state. Its length is the freight charged as a percentage of the products' price in that state.
- Worked example: MA is 26.3%. For every $100 of products customers in MA bought, $26.30 of shipping was charged on top. The national average is 16.6%, so the same $100 of products costs about $16.60 to ship on average. MA customers pay roughly $10 more per $100.
- The chart shows only the 8 states with the highest freight share, and only states with 100+ orders, so a state with 5 orders can't take the top spot by luck.

- Two things the chart doesn't tell you:

- It doesn't show how big the state is. SP isn't in the list because its freight share is low, but it brings in the most revenue. A long bar means expensive shipping, not a big market. The tooltip (hover) gives the order count.
- It doesn't tell you why. Distance is a likely reason, but nothing on this page proves it. We test it on the delivery page.
- The three key takeaways

- 1. "Top 10 categories generate 62% of revenue"

- A small number of categories carries most of the business, which is called concentration.
- A business reads it two ways: these categories deserve stock, promotion and attention, but the company also depends on them. If one has a bad month, revenue feels it.
- It recalculates with your slicers, so for one state it's that state's top 10.

- 2. "Highest avg price: computers ($1,099)"

- This is the most expensive category per item sold, not the biggest one. Computers isn't in your top 10 by revenue (look at the table), so it must sell few items.
- The point: high price doesn't mean high revenue. It's a premium niche. A good follow-up question is how many computers were actually sold, and whether that's worth growing.

- 3. "Highest freight share: MA (26.3% of revenue)"

- Customers in Maranhão pay the most for shipping relative to what they buy.
- A business reads it as a possible pricing or logistics problem: maybe delivery there is costly, or high shipping costs put people off buying.
- One honest limit: your data only contains orders that were delivered. People who left because shipping was too expensive never appear, so the real effect could be larger than the data shows.
